    Rethinking curriculum and assessment for GenAI

    How engineering curricula have responded to generative AI so far, and two approaches to assessment that keep learning visible.

    1. 1:10

      From Execution to Governance: A Systematic Analysis of Engineering Curricula in the GenAI Era

      Stella PengAAEE 2026

      Read abstractHide abstract

      Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is reshaping engineering practice, shifting professional roles from technical execution toward the orchestration and governance of AI-assisted workflows. This study conducts a systematic analysis of Australian university curricula to investigate how engineering programs have adapted to emerging GenAI competency requirements between a 2023 pre-GenAI baseline and 2026. Publicly available curriculum documents from civil, mechanical and software engineering programs at three Group of Eight universities were compared between a 2023 baseline and 2026. A comparative thematic analysis mapped structural changes across course structures, learning outcomes, graduate attributes and assessment tasks. In addition, 915 learning outcomes were systematically coded using a novel Execution-Orchestration-Governance (EOG) framework to understand the capabilities emphasised in the contemporary subjects. Institutional responses were predominantly defensive: assessment redesign prioritized supervised examinations, interactive oral assessments, and in-class activities to protect academic integrity, rather than systematically developing AI-integrated capabilities. Across the curricula, 63.8% of learning outcomes emphasized execution, 33.1% orchestration, and only 3.1% governance. Explicit AI literacy and human-AI collaboration were largely absent. These findings indicate that engineering programs remain oriented toward manual, rule-based capabilities vulnerable to automation. Faculties should therefore proactively redesign curricula to cultivate resilient human-AI orchestration and sociotechnical governance capabilities.

    2. 1:25

      Making IOAs Work at Scale: An Evidence-Based Framework from Six Semesters of Implementation

      Hasti SamadiASCILITE 2026

      Read abstractHide abstract

      The widespread availability of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has increased demand for assessment approaches that provide stronger assurance of student learning while maintaining authentic educational value. Interactive Oral Assessments (IOAs) have emerged as a promising solution, but practical guidance for implementing them sustainably in large undergraduate subjects remains limited. This paper proposes a framework grounded in six consecutive semesters of continuous IOA implementation in a large undergraduate computing subject enrolling between 300 and 560 students per offering. Evidence draws on cohort mark distributions, IOA–examination correlations, academic integrity outcomes, and examiners’ consistency, as reflected in inter-rater reliability data. Repeated iterations of IOA design, delivery, evaluation, and refinement, informed by the literature and feedback from students and staff, produced four design principles critical to successful implementation of IOA at scale. These principles address examiner calibration, rubric design, question depth and individual mark weighting. They synthesise into an operational blueprint covering assessment architecture, rubric design, question development, examiner preparation, and student preparation. Together, these provide transferable guidance for institutions seeking to implement IOA sustainably at scale in the GenAI era.

    3. 1:40

      The AI Critical Engagement Framework: Measuring Epistemic Agency and Process Accountability in Open Assessments

      Winn ChowAAEE 2026

      Read abstractHide abstract

      Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) challenge traditional higher education assessments by allowing students to produce polished work with minimal effort, increasing the risk of covert cognitive offloading and skill degradation. Preserving open assessment requires process-level transparency, yet empirical research in this area remains sparse. This study investigates student epistemic agency in a GenAI-ubiquitous environment using an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design with 121 graduate students. Students completed a case analysis report where the final product and a human-AI collaborative process audit were weighted equally (50% each). Using a quantitative Spearman’s rank correlation and a qualitative deductive thematic analysis based on the newly introduced AI Critical Engagement (ACE) framework, the research evaluated student audit trails. The findings reveal a moderate positive correlation between rigorous process accountability and final report quality. Furthermore, students strategically shifted their epistemic agency across tasks, reserving high cognitive effort for theory-driven synthesis while delegating foundational reading and formatting. The study also exposes an “Orchestration Divide,” where students who avoided GenAI or used it only for surface edits were structurally disadvantaged. Ultimately, dual-focus assessments effectively mitigate cognitive offloading, highlighting the need to formalise AI literacy within disciplinary curricula.

    1. 2:35

      A Structured Human-AI Workflow and Expert-Reviewed Rubric for Developing Pedagogically Aligned Information Systems Teaching Cases

      Vi TruongAAEE 2026

      Read abstractHide abstract

      Developing authentic, up-to-date teaching cases is time-consuming, particularly in fast-changing fields such as Information Systems and IT management. This study explores how generative AI can support educators in producing pedagogically aligned teaching cases through a structured, human-in-the-loop process. We first developed an expert-reviewed evaluation rubric covering seven dimensions of case quality, informed by the literature and case-publishing guidelines. We then designed a three-stage workflow combining topic scoping, targeted research, modular drafting, and iterative human feedback. Using this workflow, we produced two full-length case drafts in under four hours each. In a small, source-blinded assessment, two expert evaluators rated these cases alongside two selected published, human-authored cases. The AI-assisted cases received comparable ratings on the rubric, with strengths in case structure and the presentation of dilemmas. However, weaknesses remained in writing style, narrative cohesion, and access to insider organisational detail. The findings suggest that generative AI can accelerate teaching-case development, but human judgement remains essential. Further classroom evaluation is needed to establish educational effectiveness.

    2. 2:50

      Large Language Model Generation of Plausible Semantic Distractors for Data Science Parsons Problems

      Geela CheeASCILITE 2026

      Read abstractHide abstract

      Parsons problems support novice programmers by reducing the cognitive demands of writing code from scratch, requiring learners to rearrange provided blocks. Variations with distractors include erroneous blocks that do not belong in the correct solution. Although widely studied in introductory programming, Parsons problems remain underexplored in data science education, where learners may have statistical knowledge but limited programming experience. Creating effective distractors is also time-consuming for educators. This study investigated whether a large language model (LLM) could generate semantic distractors for data science Parsons problems. Distractors were generated for 315 Python-based problems and evaluated for pedagogical plausibility, compilability and output divergence. Validated distractors were then used as exemplars in a refined prompt to generate a second set, assessed using the same criteria. Results show that LLMs can generate effective semantic distractors and that incorporating validated exemplars further improves quality. The study contributes to research on data science Parsons problems, distractor design and LLM-supported educational development.

    3. 3:05

      From Awareness to Action: A Practical Model for Inclusive Teamwork and Leadership in Engineering Education

      Zoey LiAAEE 2026

      Read abstractHide abstract

      Team-based learning is central to engineering education, yet students from diverse backgrounds often encounter overt and unintentional dynamics that undermine their learning and belonging. Effective teamwork requires managing diverse perspectives and interpersonal challenges, but few structured methods foster inclusive teamwork and leadership. This practice paper documents the design, rationale, and initial implementation of a two-workshop series for engineering and information technology students at an Australian university. The intervention reframes inclusive collaboration as a set of teachable competencies, showing how structured co-curricular activities can help students move from awareness of inclusion principles to confident application in professional teamwork and leadership.
